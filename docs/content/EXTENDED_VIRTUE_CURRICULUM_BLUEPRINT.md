# Catholic Virtue Curriculum Blueprint

**Artifact type:** curriculum and content plan only - no application implementation
**Audience:** an adult learner who may have no prior knowledge of Catholic moral theology
**Editorial status:** research-backed draft requiring qualified Catholic review before publication
**Proposed scope:** one foundation course, seven counter-virtue courses, and four cardinal-virtue courses, 121 lessons total

## Executive decision

The Virtues section should feel like a guided Catholic formation library, not a tracker with short definitions attached. The learner should first understand what virtue is, why virtue matters, how grace and human effort cooperate, and how virtues and vices shape a life. The library should then contain exactly the two formation tracks requested: seven virtues taught as remedies to the seven capital sins, and the four cardinal virtues.

The approved organization is:

1. **Start Here: Becoming the Person God Calls You to Be** - an eleven-lesson foundation course. It explains the Catholic map but is not counted as a virtue.
2. **Seven Virtues against the Seven Capital Sins** - seven extended courses organized around conversion from a capital vice to a contrary way of life.
3. **The Four Cardinal Virtues** - Prudence, Justice, Fortitude, and Temperance, taught as the four pivotal moral virtues around which the other moral virtues are grouped.

Temperance appears in both tracks because it is one of the four cardinal virtues and the traditional remedy for gluttony. It must never be described as two different virtues. The two courses examine the same virtue from different angles: the cardinal course gives its complete moral framework, while the counter-vice course applies it specifically to gluttony, consumption, fasting, gratitude, and care for created goods.

The curriculum must never combine the two tracks into a newly invented list or describe the section as containing “ten virtues.” It contains a **seven-course counter-vice track** and a **four-course cardinal track**, with Temperance encountered in both for distinct pedagogical purposes.

## Doctrinal structure

### The seven capital sins and their contrary formation virtues

The Catechism formally names pride, avarice, envy, wrath, lust, gluttony, and sloth or acedia as the seven capital sins because they generate other sins and vices. Catholic catechesis has traditionally taught healing virtues against them. The exact English names in one-to-one charts vary across catechetical sources, so this curriculum should not claim that the following table is a separately defined dogmatic list. It is a faithful Catholic formation structure grounded in the Catechism, Scripture, the spiritual tradition, and the Church's teaching on each vice and virtue.

| Capital sin | Virtue taught against it | Catholic formation emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Pride | **Humility** | Truth before God: creaturely dignity, dependence, gratitude, teachability, and service |
| Avarice | **Charity expressed through generosity** | Detachment, stewardship, justice, almsgiving, trust, and love of God and neighbor |
| Envy | **Kindness and goodwill** | Rejoicing in another's good, gratitude, honor, encouragement, and fraternal love |
| Wrath | **Patience and meekness** | Governing anger, refusing vengeance, truthful reconciliation, endurance, and just action without hatred |
| Lust | **Chastity** | Integration of sexuality, self-mastery, fidelity, friendship, and authentic self-gift |
| Gluttony | **Temperance** | Gratitude, right measure, fasting, freedom in appetite, hospitality, and responsible consumption |
| Sloth or acedia | **Diligence and perseverance** | Care for the good, fidelity to duty, spiritual steadfastness, work, rest, and hope |

These courses must teach the vice accurately as well as the virtue. “Capital” does not mean that every instance is automatically a mortal sin; it means that the vice can become a source from which many other sins arise. Lessons must still teach the Catholic conditions of moral responsibility and avoid diagnosing guilt from behavior alone.

### The four cardinal virtues

The Catechism explicitly identifies four virtues as cardinal or “pivotal”:

- **Prudence** discerns the true good and chooses fitting means.
- **Justice** gives God and neighbor what is due.
- **Fortitude** supplies firmness in difficulty and constancy in pursuing the good.
- **Temperance** moderates attraction to pleasure and provides balance in the use of created goods.

All other moral virtues are grouped around these four. This cardinal track therefore supplies the moral architecture that supports the seven counter-virtue courses.

### Boundaries of this curriculum

Faith, hope, and charity must still be explained accurately because they are the three theological virtues and they animate Christian moral life. However, this curriculum does not add separate Faith and Hope courses beyond the requested tracks. Charity appears in the counter-vice track because love of God and neighbor, concretely expressed through generosity and detachment, heals the possessiveness of avarice. Likewise, gifts and fruits of the Holy Spirit should be explained where relevant but must not be confused with the two course lists.

## Why a person should learn the virtues

Virtue education should begin with desire, not accusation. Most beginners hear “virtue” and imagine rules, personality traits, moral superiority, or joyless self-control. The course should replace those images with a richer Catholic vision.

- **Virtue answers the question of happiness.** Every person seeks a good life. Catholic teaching directs that desire toward beatitude in God rather than toward pleasure, status, achievement, or control as final ends.
- **Virtue turns isolated good intentions into dependable character.** A virtue is a stable readiness to recognize and choose the good. It helps love become reliable when feelings, convenience, or social pressure change.
- **Virtue expands freedom.** Freedom is more than having options. Repeated good choices make a person increasingly capable of choosing what is true, just, and loving instead of being governed by fear, appetite, anger, habit, or approval.
- **Virtue integrates the whole person.** Catholic formation does not aim to erase emotion or the body. It educates reason, will, passions, relationships, and bodily life so they can move together toward the good.
- **Virtue makes faith visible.** Prayer, worship, and belief should become truthfulness, courage, restraint, patience, justice, service, and love in daily life.
- **Virtue is social.** Character affects families, friendships, workplaces, parishes, and civic life. Justice, truthful speech, faithful work, restraint, courage, and charity build a common life in which people can flourish.
- **Virtue prepares a person for difficulty.** Good intentions are easiest when nothing is costly. Prudence, fortitude, patience, and hope help the learner remain faithful under confusion, temptation, suffering, delay, and opposition.
- **Virtue is not self-salvation.** Human habits matter, but Christian growth depends upon grace. The Holy Spirit heals and elevates human effort; prayer, Scripture, the sacraments, community, repentance, and mercy belong inside the curriculum rather than being optional decorations.
- **Virtue is ultimately personal imitation.** The goal is not an abstractly impressive character. It is increasing conformity to Jesus Christ and freedom to love God and neighbor.

The learner should finish the orientation understanding this central sentence: **Virtue is the grace-supported formation of a free person who can recognize, choose, and increasingly delight in the good for love of God and neighbor.**

## Beginner-first course experience

Every lesson should use the same predictable sequence so the learner spends attention on the subject rather than learning a new interface.

1. **Opening question:** one ordinary-life situation that reveals why the lesson matters.
2. **Learning goal:** one sentence explaining what the learner will understand by the end.
3. **Core teaching:** approximately 800-1,200 words, divided into two or three clear ideas.
4. **Terms to know:** no more than four, defined in plain language.
5. **Read in Scripture:** one primary passage in context and up to two supporting references.
6. **Look at Christ or a biblical witness:** show the virtue embodied, not merely defined.
7. **Misconception check:** distinguish the virtue from a common counterfeit or distortion.
8. **Case study:** a realistic decision in family, work, digital life, prayer, sexuality, conflict, or service.
9. **Practice:** one concrete action small enough to attempt before the next lesson.
10. **Remember:** three retrieval questions and a concise summary statement.
11. **Prayer:** a short prayer asking for grace rather than treating the lesson as self-improvement alone.

Completion should mean **“I studied this lesson”**, never “I possess this virtue.” Knowledge checks can verify definitions and judgment in scenarios. They must not generate a holiness score, personality label, moral grade, public rank, or prediction about the learner's soul.

## Recommended pace

Each ten-lesson course is a five-week plan with two lessons per week. A lesson should take roughly 20-25 minutes, with an optional practice between lessons. The complete orientation, seven counter-virtue courses, and four cardinal courses form a path of roughly fifteen months at that pace. Only the orientation should be required. Afterward, learners may begin the counter-vice track or the cardinal track according to their formation needs.

At the end of each course, offer an optional 30-day practice period. That practice period should revisit the course's final rule of life, not introduce new doctrine.

# Orientation Course - Becoming the Person God Calls You to Be

**Purpose:** Give a complete beginner the map needed to understand every course that follows.
**Outcome:** The learner can explain virtue, distinguish human and theological virtues, understand the roles of freedom, passions, habit, vice, grace, Scripture, sacraments, and community, and create a humane plan for continued study.

## Lesson plan

1. **The happiness beneath every choice**
   - **Teach:** Every deliberate choice reaches for some perceived good. Christian moral life begins by asking what human beings are made for. Beatitude in God does not reject created goods; it places them in their proper order.
   - **Scripture:** Matthew 5:1-12; Psalm 16; Matthew 6:19-34.
   - **Practice:** List five things currently pursued as sources of happiness and identify which are means, partial goods, or final ends.

2. **What virtue actually is**
   - **Teach:** A virtue is a habitual and firm disposition to do the good. Distinguish knowledge, intention, isolated action, temperament, skill, reputation, and stable character. Explain Rickaby's skill analogy while preserving freedom and grace.
   - **Scripture:** Philippians 4:8-9; Matthew 7:24-27.
   - **Practice:** Name one good action that has become easier through repetition and one good still dependent on mood.

3. **Freedom for the good**
   - **Teach:** Catholic freedom is not mere choice among options. The repeated choice of the good enlarges freedom, while vice narrows it. Responsibility depends on what is genuinely known and voluntarily chosen.
   - **Scripture:** John 8:31-36; Romans 6:12-23; Sirach 15:14-17.
   - **Practice:** Rewrite one “I have no choice” statement by naming the actual constraints and the smallest real freedom still available.

4. **Feelings, temptation, and consent**
   - **Teach:** Passions are not automatically virtuous or sinful. Strong feelings, bodily reactions, intrusive thoughts, temptation, and deliberate consent are different realities. Formation should order the passions without creating scrupulosity.
   - **Scripture:** Mark 14:32-42; 1 Corinthians 10:13; Psalm 42.
   - **Practice:** Observe one strong emotion using four questions: What happened? What do I feel? What good is at stake? What action is truly mine?

5. **How repeated acts become habits - for good or ill**
   - **Teach:** Good acts are both fruit and seed of character. Repeated sin can become vice and cloud judgment. Habit explains influence, not automatic guilt; repentance, repair, new supports, and renewed action can interrupt a pattern.
   - **Scripture:** Galatians 6:7-10; Hebrews 12:1-13; Luke 15:11-32.
   - **Practice:** Draw one habit loop: cue, usual response, apparent reward, deeper cost, and a better response.

6. **The four hinges of moral life**
   - **Teach:** Prudence sees and chooses fitting means; justice gives what is due; fortitude remains firm when the good is difficult; temperance orders desire. Show why each virtue needs the other three.
   - **Scripture:** Wisdom 8:7; Micah 6:8; Titus 2:11-14.
   - **Practice:** Analyze one decision four ways: What is wise? What is owed? What difficulty must be faced? What desire needs order?

7. **Faith, hope, and charity: life received from God**
   - **Teach:** The theological virtues are infused by God and relate directly to him. Faith receives truth, hope trusts God's promise and help, and charity loves God above all and neighbor in God. Charity gives Christian form to every moral virtue.
   - **Scripture:** 1 Corinthians 13; Hebrews 11:1-12:3; Romans 5:1-5.
   - **Practice:** Identify where knowledge needs faith, discouragement needs hope, or correct behavior needs charity.

8. **Grace, gifts, fruits, and cooperation**
   - **Teach:** Human effort is real but cannot save or perfect itself. Grace heals and elevates; the Holy Spirit makes believers docile to divine prompting; the fruits of the Spirit reveal a life being formed from within.
   - **Scripture:** John 15:1-17; Galatians 5:16-25; Romans 8:1-17.
   - **Practice:** Pair one concrete effort with a specific prayer for grace and one source of human support.

9. **How Christ, the Church, and the sacraments form virtue**
   - **Teach:** Jesus is the living pattern of virtue. Scripture corrects the imagination, the Eucharist joins worship to self-gift, Reconciliation restores communion, prayer disposes the heart, and community prevents isolated self-invention.
   - **Scripture:** John 13:1-17; Luke 24:13-35; Acts 2:42-47.
   - **Practice:** Build a weekly formation rhythm containing Scripture, prayer, worship, relationship, and one deliberate act.

10. **A lifelong school of love**
   - **Teach:** Virtue does not graduate into mastery. Growth includes seasons, failure, mercy, repair, changed circumstances, and deeper surrender. Introduce the full library and help the learner choose a next course for a reason rather than from shame.
   - **Scripture:** Philippians 3:7-16; 2 Peter 1:3-11; Matthew 11:28-30.
   - **Capstone:** Write a one-page “formation map”: desired good, present responsibility, one needed virtue, supports, foreseeable distortion, and first faithful step.

**Primary source pack:** Catechism 1691-1698, 1716-1729, 1730-1775, 1803-1845, 1865-1869, and 1996-2016; *Lumen Gentium* 39-42; *Gaudete et Exsultate* 1-34 and 65-109; Rickaby chapters I, II, V, and XII.

# The Four Cardinal Virtues

The following four courses are the complete cardinal track authorized by Catechism 1805-1809.

## Cardinal Course - Prudence: Learning to See and Choose Well

**Why it matters:** Good intentions do not guarantee good judgment. Prudence brings truth, moral principles, circumstances, counsel, foresight, and decisive action together. It is not fearfulness; it is practical wisdom in service of the true good.
**Outcome:** The learner can conduct a sound discernment, recognize counterfeits such as cunning or paralysis, and act at an appropriate time with a teachable spirit.

## Lesson plan

1. **Right reason in action**
   - Define prudence as discerning the true good and choosing fitting means. Explain why it guides the other moral virtues.
   - **Scripture:** Proverbs 14:8, 15; Matthew 7:24-27.
   - **Practice:** Separate the desired end from the proposed method in one current decision.

2. **Begin with the true good**
   - Show that efficiency cannot rescue a corrupt end. Distinguish apparent, immediate, partial, and integral goods.
   - **Scripture:** Mark 8:34-37; Luke 12:13-21.
   - **Practice:** Ask, “If this succeeds exactly as planned, what kind of good will actually exist?”

3. **Attend to reality**
   - Teach truthful memory, attention to facts, relevant experience, and freedom from wishful thinking. Discuss bias, pressure, and incomplete information without promising certainty.
   - **Scripture:** 1 Kings 3:5-14; Proverbs 18:13, 17.
   - **Practice:** Make two columns: known facts and untested interpretations.

4. **Docility and counsel**
   - Present teachability as intellectual humility, not gullibility. Explain how to choose counsel that is wise, competent, morally trustworthy, and appropriately informed.
   - **Scripture:** Proverbs 15:22; Sirach 6:32-37.
   - **Practice:** Ask one well-chosen person a precise question instead of seeking general reassurance.

5. **Forming and following conscience**
   - Distinguish conscience from preference, emotion, social approval, or a private revelation. Explain the duty to form conscience through truth, Church teaching, prayer, and honest attention.
   - **Scripture:** Romans 12:1-2; Romans 14:22-23.
   - **Practice:** Name the moral principle and the concrete facts relevant to one case.

6. **Deliberation without paralysis**
   - Teach proportionate investigation, comparison of morally acceptable options, consequences, obligations, uncertainty, and the danger of demanding impossible certainty.
   - **Scripture:** Luke 14:28-33; James 1:5-8.
   - **Practice:** Set a decision deadline appropriate to the stakes and list what information would actually change the choice.

7. **Judgment, command, and timely action**
   - Prudence is incomplete until judgment becomes action. Address procrastination, perfectionism, impulsiveness, and the humility to revise after acting.
   - **Scripture:** Matthew 25:1-13; James 1:22-25.
   - **Practice:** Take the smallest action that honestly commits to the sound judgment already reached.

8. **Foresight, timing, and gradualness**
   - Teach anticipation of foreseeable effects, care for people affected, respect for process, and Pope Francis's warning that excess zeal can damage a good work that needs gradualness.
   - **Scripture:** Genesis 41:33-40, 47-57; Proverbs 22:3.
   - **Practice:** Write a pre-mortem: “If this good plan failed through my choices, what likely went wrong?”

9. **Counterfeit prudence**
   - Contrast prudence with timidity, respectability, indecision, clever self-interest, manipulation, rationalization, and rash confidence. Pair wisdom with innocence and justice.
   - **Scripture:** Matthew 10:16; Luke 16:1-13.
   - **Practice:** Identify which counterfeit most often borrows the name of wisdom in the learner's life.

10. **Capstone: a complete discernment**
   - Apply the sequence: name the good, identify duties, gather facts, seek counsel, pray, generate morally sound options, judge, act, and review.
   - **Scripture:** Psalm 119:97-112; Philippians 1:9-11.
   - **Capstone:** Complete a written discernment for one proportionate real-life decision and schedule a review after action.

**Primary source pack:** Catechism 1776-1802 and 1806; Pope Francis, General Audience on Prudence, 20 March 2024; Rickaby chapter III; Wisdom 8; selected Proverbs and Gospel parables.

## Cardinal Course - Justice: Giving God and Neighbor What Is Due

**Why it matters:** Justice makes love truthful in obligations, rights, speech, property, work, worship, repair, and the common good. Without it, strength governs the weak and charity becomes sentiment.
**Outcome:** The learner can recognize what is due, honor the dignity and rights of others, repair concrete wrongs, and connect personal conduct with the common good.

## Lesson plan

1. **The firm will to give what is due**
   - Define justice and explain “due” in relation to dignity, rights, roles, promises, law, gratitude, and the common good.
   - **Scripture:** Micah 6:8; Matthew 5:6; Psalm 15.
   - **Practice:** Identify one person or community to whom something specific is presently owed.

2. **Justice toward God: the virtue of religion**
   - Explain worship, gratitude, prayer, fidelity, and reverence as justice toward God while clarifying that grace itself can never be earned.
   - **Scripture:** Deuteronomy 6:4-9; Luke 17:11-19; Romans 12:1.
   - **Practice:** Restore one neglected act of worship or gratitude for the right reason.

3. **Every person's God-given dignity**
   - Ground rights and duties in the person rather than usefulness, status, productivity, agreement, or affection. Introduce equality, legitimate difference, and special care for vulnerability.
   - **Scripture:** Genesis 1:26-27; James 2:1-9; Matthew 25:31-46.
   - **Practice:** Notice where convenience or group loyalty has obscured someone's equal dignity.

4. **Truth, speech, and reputation**
   - Teach honesty, appropriate discretion, promises, gossip, detraction, calumny, rash judgment, and the harm of half-truths. Justice protects both truth and a person's good name.
   - **Scripture:** Matthew 5:33-37; James 3:1-12; Ephesians 4:25-32.
   - **Practice:** Correct, retract, or refuse to repeat one unfair statement.

5. **Promises, duties, property, and stewardship**
   - Cover keeping one's word, returning what is borrowed, respecting property, honoring contracts, paying debts, and using possessions with regard for the universal destination of goods.
   - **Scripture:** Exodus 20:15-17; Leviticus 19:11-13; Luke 16:10-13.
   - **Practice:** Close one concrete obligation: return, repay, deliver, clarify, or renegotiate honestly.

6. **Restitution, apology, and repair**
   - Explain why regret alone may be insufficient when harm can be repaired. Distinguish apology, restitution, changed behavior, consequences, forgiveness, and reconciliation.
   - **Scripture:** Luke 19:1-10; Matthew 5:23-24; Numbers 5:5-8.
   - **Practice:** Draft a repair plan that does not pressure the harmed person to comfort or absolve the offender.

7. **Work, wages, authority, and responsibility**
   - Apply justice to employees, employers, customers, leadership, fair wages, honest work, exploitation, safety, credit, and the dignity of labor.
   - **Scripture:** James 5:1-6; Colossians 3:22-4:1; Deuteronomy 24:14-15.
   - **Practice:** Examine whether the learner's work gives honest value and whether the learner's demands allow others human dignity and rest.

8. **Social justice, solidarity, and the common good**
   - Move beyond private fairness to structures, participation, law, the poor, civic responsibility, and social conditions in which persons and communities can flourish.
   - **Scripture:** Amos 5:21-24; Isaiah 58:6-12; Acts 4:32-35.
   - **Practice:** Choose one informed civic or communal responsibility instead of stopping at private concern.

9. **Justice, mercy, and charity**
   - Show that charity fulfills and exceeds justice but never excuses theft, deception, abuse, exploitation, or unpaid debt. Mercy seeks the true good without denying truth or accountability.
   - **Scripture:** Luke 10:25-37; John 8:1-11; Matthew 23:23.
   - **Practice:** Analyze a case by asking separately: What does justice require? What can mercy freely add?

10. **Capstone: a justice audit**
   - Review worship, speech, promises, money, work, power, repair, and participation in the common good.
   - **Scripture:** Tobit 4:5-11; Proverbs 21:3, 21; Matthew 7:12.
   - **Capstone:** Choose one overdue act of justice, define the safe and proportionate repair, seek counsel if needed, and complete the first step.

**Primary source pack:** Catechism 1807, 1928-1942, 2095-2109, 2401-2463, and 2464-2513; Pope Francis, General Audience on Justice, 3 April 2024; Rickaby chapters VII-X; the prophetic books, Tobit, the Gospels, and James.

## Cardinal Course - Fortitude: Courage for the Difficult Good

**Why it matters:** Knowledge of the good does not remove fear, cost, opposition, suffering, or weariness. Fortitude carries a prudent and just choice through difficulty without becoming rash, hard, or self-sufficient.
**Outcome:** The learner can distinguish fortitude from fearlessness, aggression, and stubbornness; respond proportionately to internal and external obstacles; and seek help without shame.

## Lesson plan

1. **What courage is - and is not**
   - Define fortitude as firmness in difficulty and constancy in the pursuit of the good. Fear can be present and informative; courage keeps it from ruling the choice.
   - **Scripture:** Joshua 1:1-9; Psalm 27.
   - **Practice:** Name the feared outcome, the good at stake, and what fear is accurately revealing.

2. **A worthy cause and a right motive**
   - Difficulty alone does not make an act brave. Distinguish fidelity from vanity, anger, thrill-seeking, social pressure, pride, and needless danger.
   - **Scripture:** John 15:12-13; Matthew 6:1-6.
   - **Practice:** Ask whether the action serves a proportionate good or merely a desired self-image.

3. **Cowardice, rashness, and stubbornness**
   - Show fortitude as a mean shaped by prudence. Avoidance can betray the good; rashness ignores unnecessary harm; stubbornness refuses truth or correction.
   - **Scripture:** Sirach 3:17-29; Proverbs 27:12; Matthew 26:51-54.
   - **Practice:** Adjust one proposed act by adding preparation, counsel, or a safer proportionate step.

4. **The courage to act**
   - Explore initiative, truthful speech, intervention, protection of the vulnerable, apology, asking for help, and saying a necessary no.
   - **Scripture:** Esther 4:10-17; Acts 4:13-31; Daniel 13:41-46.
   - **Practice:** Perform one concrete act that has been delayed mainly by fear of discomfort or disapproval.

5. **The courage to endure**
   - Rickaby emphasizes endurance as fortitude's deepest form. Teach remaining faithful under pain, repetition, delay, obscurity, and discouragement without romanticizing harm.
   - **Scripture:** Romans 5:1-5; Hebrews 10:32-39; James 1:2-4.
   - **Practice:** Choose one good to continue for a defined period and identify the support that makes endurance humane.

6. **Jesus in Gethsemane and the Passion**
   - Contemplate courage that fully feels sorrow, asks for companionship, prays honestly, submits to the Father, refuses violence, and remains faithful in suffering.
   - **Scripture:** Mark 14:32-42; Luke 22:39-46; Luke 23:32-46.
   - **Practice:** Pray with one feared duty using Jesus' honest petition and surrendered trust.

7. **Moral courage in ordinary life**
   - Apply fortitude to integrity at work, resisting group pressure, defending another's reputation, maintaining boundaries, admitting fault, recovery, vocation, and unseen duty.
   - **Scripture:** Daniel 3; 2 Timothy 1:6-14.
   - **Practice:** Rehearse a truthful, non-aggressive sentence needed in one real situation.

8. **Fortitude against evil and injustice**
   - Courage sometimes requires resistance, reporting, advocacy, lawful action, or public witness. It must remain governed by justice, charity, truth, and respect for life.
   - **Scripture:** Isaiah 1:16-17; Proverbs 31:8-9; Ephesians 6:10-18.
   - **Practice:** Identify the appropriate authority, ally, or safe channel for one concern.

9. **Help, limits, and grace**
   - Receiving pastoral, medical, psychological, legal, or relational help can be fortitude. Withdrawal from danger can be courageous. Christian courage is sustained by hope, prayer, sacraments, community, and providence.
   - **Scripture:** Exodus 17:8-13; 1 Kings 19:1-18; 2 Corinthians 12:7-10.
   - **Practice:** Make one specific request for support instead of treating self-sufficiency as bravery.

10. **Capstone: one courageous fidelity**
   - Integrate the good sought, fear, motive, proportionality, preparation, support, action, endurance, and review.
   - **Scripture:** 1 Corinthians 16:13-14; Psalm 31:20-25.
   - **Capstone:** Create and carry out a graduated courage plan with a small, medium, and only-if-needed larger step.

**Primary source pack:** Catechism 1808; Pope Francis, General Audience on Fortitude, 10 April 2024; *Gaudete et Exsultate* 112-139; Rickaby chapter VI; biblical witnesses including Esther, Susanna, the apostles, and Christ's Passion.

## Cardinal Course - Temperance: Freedom, Measure, and Gratitude

**Why it matters:** Created goods are real goods, yet appetite can narrow attention until the immediate pleasure obscures vocation, health, relationship, worship, and freedom. Temperance restores order so a person can enjoy, limit, fast from, or refuse a good for the sake of something greater.
**Outcome:** The learner can understand desire without shame, discern a prudent measure, use ascetic practices for love rather than control, and seek appropriate care when a problem exceeds spiritual self-help.

## Lesson plan

1. **Created goods and ordered desire**
   - Begin with creation's goodness. Temperance is not contempt for the body, pleasure, food, rest, sexuality, celebration, or material things; it orders their use toward integral flourishing.
   - **Scripture:** Genesis 1:26-31; 1 Timothy 4:1-5; Psalm 104.
   - **Practice:** Receive one ordinary pleasure slowly, gratefully, and without distraction.

2. **Self-mastery as freedom**
   - Explain the difference between repression, indulgence, and integration. The goal is not feeling nothing but becoming able to choose rather than obey every impulse.
   - **Scripture:** 1 Corinthians 6:12-20; Sirach 18:30-33.
   - **Practice:** Insert a sixty-second free pause before one habitual consumption choice.

3. **The prudent measure**
   - Temperance is not always “less.” The fitting measure depends on the good, person, duties, health, vocation, and circumstance; some goods require complete abstinence for some people.
   - **Scripture:** Proverbs 25:16, 27-28; Philippians 4:10-13.
   - **Practice:** Define “enough” for one domain and state the greater good the limit protects.

4. **Food, drink, gluttony, and festivity**
   - Treat nourishment, hospitality, fasting, celebration, excess, haste, waste, and using consumption to escape. Avoid equating body size, appetite, or enjoyment with moral failure.
   - **Scripture:** Deuteronomy 8:7-18; Luke 7:33-34; Philippians 3:17-21.
   - **Practice:** Eat or drink one chosen item with attention to gratitude, need, companionship, and stopping freely.

5. **Attention, entertainment, and digital appetite**
   - Apply temperance to feeds, video, gaming, news, notifications, and novelty. The concern is not technology itself but whether attention remains available for God, people, work, and rest.
   - **Scripture:** Ephesians 5:15-17; Psalm 101:1-3; Mark 4:18-20.
   - **Practice:** Establish one time, place, or notification boundary and replace it with a named greater good.

6. **Possessions, spending, and consumption**
   - Explore acquisition, convenience, comparison, debt, waste, generosity, care for creation, and the relationship between temperance and justice.
   - **Scripture:** Luke 12:13-34; 1 Timothy 6:6-10, 17-19.
   - **Practice:** Delay one nonessential purchase and examine the desire beneath it.

7. **Speech, anger, and the free pause**
   - Extend self-command to reacting, interrupting, posting, venting, sarcasm, and the pleasure of having the last word. Temperance makes space for prudence, justice, patience, and kindness.
   - **Scripture:** James 1:19-21; Proverbs 15:1-4; Sirach 5:11-6:1.
   - **Practice:** Use a prepared pause sentence before responding in one charged conversation.

8. **Fasting, abstinence, feasting, and rest**
   - Place Christian asceticism inside prayer, almsgiving, liturgical life, gratitude, and solidarity. Include Sabbath rest and feasting so discipline does not become permanent austerity or self-punishment.
   - **Scripture:** Matthew 6:16-18; Isaiah 58:3-12; Mark 2:18-22.
   - **Practice:** Choose one safe, proportionate fast joined to prayer and a concrete act of charity.

9. **When self-help is not enough**
   - Distinguish ordinary appetite from addiction, compulsion, disordered eating, trauma responses, and medical concerns. Qualified care, accountability, and environmental change can be part of virtue.
   - **Scripture:** Mark 2:15-17; Galatians 6:1-5.
   - **Practice:** Identify one concern that deserves pastoral, medical, psychological, or recovery support rather than a harsher private rule.

10. **Capstone: a humane rule of enough**
   - Integrate gratitude, purpose, measure, triggers, environment, replacement goods, support, mercy after failure, and periodic review.
   - **Scripture:** Titus 2:11-14; 1 Corinthians 9:24-27; Galatians 5:22-25.
   - **Capstone:** Write a four-week rule for one domain that is positive, specific, safe, revisable, and ordered toward love.

**Primary source pack:** Catechism 1809, 2043, 2184-2188, and 2288-2291; Pope Francis, General Audience on Temperance, 17 April 2024, and catechesis on Gluttony, 10 January 2024; Rickaby chapter IV; Wisdom and Pauline teaching on sobriety and self-control.

# The Seven Virtues against the Seven Capital Sins

These seven courses teach conversion from a capital vice toward its contrary virtue. They are not a substitute for the cardinal structure above; they apply Catholic doctrine to seven especially generative patterns of sin.

## Counter-Virtue Course - Chastity against Lust: Integrated Love and the Gift of the Body

**Why it matters:** Lust turns sexual desire toward possession or consumption of another person, separating pleasure from the person's full dignity and the moral meaning of self-gift. Chastity is often reduced to prohibitions, but Catholic teaching presents it positively as the successful integration of sexuality within the whole person. It belongs to every state of life, not only to unmarried people.
**Outcome:** The learner can distinguish lust from attraction and authentic love, explain chastity positively, distinguish temptation from consent and action, recognize love that honors the whole person, adopt wise boundaries, and approach failure or woundedness through truth, mercy, and appropriate help.

## Lesson plan

1. **The body and sexuality as gifts**
   - Begin with the goodness of creation and the dignity of the embodied person. Sexuality is not a dirty compartment of life; it concerns the whole person and the human capacity for relationship, covenant, and self-gift.
   - **Scripture:** Genesis 1:26-31; Genesis 2:18-25; Psalm 139:13-18.
   - **Practice:** Replace one contemptuous or objectifying thought about a body—one's own or another's—with a statement of personal dignity.

2. **Integration rather than repression or indulgence**
   - Define chastity using Catechism 2337. Contrast integration with pretending desire does not exist, treating desire as command, and dividing sexual behavior from the good of the whole person.
   - **Scripture:** 1 Corinthians 6:12-20; Romans 12:1-2; 1 Thessalonians 4:1-8.
   - **Practice:** Map one recurring desire through body, emotion, imagination, relationship, responsibility, and ultimate good.

3. **Self-mastery and the freedom to love**
   - Teach self-mastery as a long, gradual work, not a one-time victory. The aim is the freedom to make a sincere gift of self. Environment, habits, friendships, prayer, sacraments, and grace all matter.
   - **Scripture:** Sirach 18:30-33; Matthew 5:8; Galatians 5:13-25.
   - **Practice:** Create one modest environmental boundary and one positive replacement action for a predictable moment of vulnerability.

4. **One virtue, different states of life**
   - Explain how chastity takes distinct forms in marriage, engagement, single life, celibacy, widowhood, and seasons of separation or illness. Everyone is called to fidelity and to treating persons as persons, never as instruments.
   - **Scripture:** Matthew 19:3-12; 1 Corinthians 7:1-9, 32-35; Tobit 8:4-9.
   - **Practice:** Describe what faithful love requires in the learner's present state of life, without making promises about an unknown future.

5. **Love, attraction, possession, and objectification**
   - Attraction can recognize beauty; lust takes or imagines taking another person for gratification. Explore the difference between mutual self-gift and possession, and between knowing a person and consuming an image of one.
   - **Scripture:** Song of Songs 8:6-7; Matthew 5:27-30; 1 Corinthians 13:4-7.
   - **Practice:** In one interaction, deliberately attend to the other person's story, agency, responsibilities, and eternal dignity.

6. **Temptation, consent, habit, and responsibility**
   - Distinguish an unwanted thought, spontaneous attraction, temptation, entertained fantasy, consent, and outward action. Explain that freedom and responsibility can be affected by habit, fear, immaturity, trauma, or compulsion without erasing the call to truthful action and help.
   - **Scripture:** Hebrews 4:14-16; 1 Corinthians 10:12-13; James 1:12-16.
   - **Practice:** Write a non-scrupulous response sequence: notice, name, refuse or redirect, seek grace, and continue the next good action.

7. **Friendship, affection, loneliness, and community**
   - Chastity grows through healthy friendship, appropriate affection, shared mission, family and community life, not merely avoidance. Treat loneliness honestly so sexual temptation is not addressed only at the level of willpower.
   - **Scripture:** Sirach 6:5-17; John 15:9-17; Acts 2:42-47.
   - **Practice:** Initiate one healthy, non-transactional connection or ask for companionship in a lonely setting.

8. **Digital life, pornography, and protective boundaries**
   - Explain how pornography trains objectification, secrecy, escalation, and isolation. Pair moral clarity with practical safeguards and a path to recovery. Do not promise that filters or private resolve alone will heal compulsion.
   - **Scripture:** Job 31:1; Psalm 101:1-3; Matthew 5:27-30.
   - **Practice:** Establish one device or location boundary and identify one trustworthy person, recovery group, counselor, or pastoral resource if secrecy has become entrenched.

9. **Covenant, fidelity, and responsible self-gift**
   - Place Catholic sexual morality within the goods it protects: the whole person, covenant fidelity, permanence, mutual self-gift, openness to life, children, family, and the inseparability of body and promise. Present teaching clearly without treating people as case studies or reducing marriage to permission.
   - **Scripture:** Genesis 2:18-25; Mark 10:6-9; Ephesians 5:21-33.
   - **Practice:** Evaluate one cultural message about sex by asking what it assumes about the body, freedom, commitment, consequences, and the other person's good.

10. **Mercy, healing, and a personal integrity covenant**
   - Failure is neither harmless nor the end of the story. Teach examination without obsession, repentance, Reconciliation, repair where possible, gradual growth, and qualified care for trauma, abuse, addiction, or compulsive behavior. Forgiveness never requires renewed exposure to abuse.
   - **Scripture:** Psalm 51; John 8:1-11; Luke 15:11-32.
   - **Capstone:** Write a personal integrity covenant covering the good being sought, boundaries, community, sacramental life, response after failure, and when to seek professional help.

**Pastoral guardrails:** Never equate temptation, involuntary bodily response, trauma, infertility, disability, marital status, or sexual history with a person's worth. Never prescribe private disclosure to an unsafe person. Abuse requires safety and competent support, not endurance framed as virtue.
**Primary source pack:** Catechism 2337-2359 and 2514-2533; Benedict XVI, *Deus Caritas Est* 2-18; Pope Francis, General Audience on Lust, 17 January 2024; *Gaudete et Exsultate* 83-86; biblical theology of creation, covenant, body, and self-gift.

## Counter-Virtue Course - Charity and Generosity against Avarice

**Why it matters:** Avarice is not defined by income or the number of possessions but by a heart grasping for ownership, security, control, or accumulation. Charity receives God's prior love and turns outward toward God and neighbor; generosity, detachment, justice, and almsgiving are concrete expressions of that love against avarice.
**Outcome:** The learner can recognize avarice beyond stereotypes, explain why charity and generosity answer it, distinguish stewardship from possessiveness, use material goods according to justice and love, practice detachment and almsgiving, and form a sustainable rule of generous charity.

## Lesson plan

1. **God loves first**
   - Christianity begins not with earning love but with receiving God's self-gift in creation, covenant, Christ, and the Holy Spirit. Human charity is an answer made possible by grace.
   - **Scripture:** John 3:16-17; Romans 5:5-8; 1 John 4:7-19.
   - **Practice:** Spend ten minutes receiving one biblical statement of God's love without turning it immediately into a task.

2. **Avarice: a sickness of the heart**
   - Define avarice as disordered attachment to wealth or possessions, not as a sin limited to rich people. It can appear as hoarding, refusal to share, dishonest gain, treating security as salvation, or clinging to objects of little value.
   - **Scripture:** Luke 12:13-21; 1 Timothy 6:6-10; Hebrews 13:5-6.
   - **Practice:** Identify one possession or financial fear that exerts disproportionate control over attention or choice.

3. **Poverty of spirit, trust, and detachment**
   - Detachment does not require contempt for property or irresponsible neglect of dependents. It means receiving goods as gifts and tools rather than foundations of identity, control, or ultimate safety.
   - **Scripture:** Matthew 5:3; Matthew 6:19-34; Philippians 4:10-13.
   - **Practice:** Complete one small act of release: lend, give, discard responsibly, or decline an unnecessary acquisition.

4. **Stewardship, private property, and the universal destination of goods**
   - Catholic teaching recognizes legitimate private property while insisting that created goods are destined for the whole human family. Ownership creates responsibility for family, neighbor, worker, community, poor, and creation.
   - **Scripture:** Genesis 1:26-31; Leviticus 19:9-10; Acts 4:32-35.
   - **Practice:** Examine one owned resource—money, space, time, skill, or equipment—and identify whom it is capable of serving.

5. **Generosity and almsgiving**
   - Christian giving answers God's generosity. Teach planned and spontaneous giving, sacrifice, anonymity, respect for recipients, prudence toward responsibilities, and the difference between giving from love and buying approval.
   - **Scripture:** Tobit 4:5-11; Mark 12:41-44; 2 Corinthians 8:7-15, 9:6-11.
   - **Practice:** Give a specific amount, object, time, or skill to a real need without seeking recognition.

6. **Justice before optional generosity**
   - Charity never excuses withholding what is due. Wages, debts, taxes, promises, care of dependents, honest contracts, restitution, and repair precede self-congratulation for discretionary giving.
   - **Scripture:** Luke 19:1-10; James 5:1-6; Romans 13:6-8.
   - **Practice:** Identify and begin one payment, return, correction, or restitution already owed.

7. **Simplicity, consumption, and freedom from comparison**
   - Examine status purchases, upgrades, clutter, debt, waste, and the use of consumption to secure identity or compete. Simplicity orders possessions to vocation and love; it is not an aesthetic available only to the comfortable.
   - **Scripture:** Luke 3:10-14; 1 Timothy 6:17-19; James 2:1-9.
   - **Practice:** Delay one nonessential purchase and direct some of its cost toward a responsibility or act of mercy.

8. **Mercy and the poor**
   - Study the corporal and spiritual works of mercy. Charity responds to concrete need while justice also asks why people lack what is due to them. Service should respect agency rather than make the giver the hero.
   - **Scripture:** Matthew 25:31-46; James 2:14-18; Tobit 4:5-11.
   - **Practice:** Complete one work of mercy selected in response to a real need, then reflect on what listening changed.

9. **Eucharistic, communal, and missionary love**
   - The Eucharist forms one body and sends disciples into love of neighbor. Charity is ecclesial as well as private: it builds communion, bears burdens, shares gifts, and witnesses to Christ.
   - **Scripture:** Acts 2:42-47; 1 Corinthians 10:16-17; John 13:1-17, 34-35.
   - **Practice:** Serve one ongoing responsibility in family, parish, or community that will still matter after the emotional moment passes.

10. **Capstone: a rule of charitable stewardship**
   - Integrate trust in God, legitimate provision, justice, detachment, almsgiving, mercy, simplicity, community, and periodic examination of attachments.
   - **Scripture:** John 15:1-17; 1 John 3:16-24; Acts 20:32-35.
   - **Capstone:** Build a four-week rule with one act of detachment, one form of planned generosity, one work of mercy, and a review of what possessions are doing to the heart.

**Primary source pack:** Catechism 1812-1829, 2401-2463, 2534-2550, and 2443-2449; Benedict XVI, *Deus Caritas Est*; Pope Francis, General Audiences on Avarice, 24 January 2024, and Charity, 15 May 2024; the Johannine writings, 1 Corinthians 13, and Christ's teaching on possessions, the poor, and love of neighbor.

## Counter-Virtue Course - Diligence against Sloth and Acedia

**Why it matters:** Diligence is more than productivity and its opposite is more than laziness. Christian diligence is faithful, attentive care for the good actually entrusted to a person. It resists acedia, avoidance, carelessness, and discouragement while also rejecting overwork, perfectionism, and the idolatry of efficiency.
**Outcome:** The learner can discern the next faithful duty, begin and persevere without worshiping output, unite work with prayer and service, honor human limits and Sunday rest, and form a humane rule of faithful work.

## Lesson plan

1. **Work, stewardship, and vocation**
   - Human work participates in caring for creation and serving others. Diligence applies not only to employment but also to study, household work, caregiving, worship, relationships, recovery, and civic responsibility.
   - **Scripture:** Genesis 2:4-15; Colossians 3:17, 22-24; Matthew 25:14-30.
   - **Practice:** Name three goods or persons served by one ordinary task.

2. **Sloth, acedia, fatigue, and incapacity**
   - Distinguish unwillingness to pursue the good from physical exhaustion, illness, depression, disability, grief, overwhelm, or the need for rest. Acedia can appear as restless busyness as well as inertia when both avoid the call of the present moment.
   - **Scripture:** 1 Kings 19:1-18; Proverbs 24:30-34; Psalm 42.
   - **Practice:** Ask whether one avoided duty needs repentance, a smaller first step, rest, assistance, treatment, or a changed expectation.

3. **Discerning the duty of the present moment**
   - Not every possible good is one's present responsibility. Prudence orders duties by vocation, promises, urgency, dependency, consequence, and capacity. Prayer keeps diligence from becoming frantic self-importance.
   - **Scripture:** Luke 10:38-42; Ecclesiastes 3:1-11; Ephesians 5:15-17.
   - **Practice:** Select today's next faithful action from obligations rather than from anxiety or novelty.

4. **Beginning despite resistance**
   - Explore procrastination driven by discomfort, ambiguity, fear, resentment, or perfectionism. Diligence begins with a concrete action small enough to do and meaningful enough to move the real duty.
   - **Scripture:** Proverbs 6:6-11; Matthew 21:28-31; Zechariah 4:10.
   - **Practice:** Define and complete a ten-minute “faithful beginning” on one avoided task.

5. **Attention in a distracted age**
   - Attention is a form of stewardship and love. Study interruption, novelty, divided presence, and environmental design without making concentration difficulties a moral verdict.
   - **Scripture:** Proverbs 4:20-27; Mark 4:1-20; Philippians 4:8-9.
   - **Practice:** Protect one focused interval and write down distractions rather than obeying them.

6. **Craftsmanship, competence, and teachability**
   - Careful work requires learning, correction, preparation, and respect for those affected by poor work. Excellence serves the good; perfectionism uses impossible standards to secure identity or avoid exposure.
   - **Scripture:** Exodus 31:1-11; Sirach 38:24-34; Proverbs 22:29.
   - **Practice:** Ask for one piece of specific feedback and make one proportionate improvement.

7. **Perseverance, finishing, and repair**
   - Teach the middle of work: returning after novelty fades, honoring deadlines and promises, repairing careless mistakes, and recognizing when responsible completion means handing off or renegotiating.
   - **Scripture:** Galatians 6:7-10; 2 Thessalonians 3:6-13; Luke 14:28-30.
   - **Practice:** Finish, repair, or responsibly renegotiate one small open obligation.

8. **Limits, perfectionism, and the idol of productivity**
   - A person's worth is not output. Jesus accepted bodily limits and withdrew to pray. Diligence does not require maximizing every hour, controlling every outcome, or neglecting people for measurable achievement.
   - **Scripture:** Psalm 127; Mark 6:30-32; Luke 12:13-21.
   - **Practice:** Define what “faithfully sufficient” looks like for one task before beginning it.

9. **Work, justice, Sabbath, and rest**
   - “Work is for man, not man for work.” Consider fair conditions, family needs, care for workers, leisure, worship, Sunday rest, and protection of those whose labor makes another's rest possible.
   - **Scripture:** Deuteronomy 5:12-15; Mark 2:23-28; James 5:1-6.
   - **Practice:** Plan a Sunday period for worship, relationship, mercy, and restorative rest while treating necessary workers justly.

10. **Capstone: a rule of faithful work**
   - Integrate vocation, priority, attention, starting, completion, limits, help, prayer, rest, and review. The rule must flex with health, season of life, and legitimate duties.
   - **Scripture:** John 15:1-8; 1 Corinthians 15:58; Psalm 90:12-17.
   - **Capstone:** Design and test a two-week rhythm containing a daily priority, focused work, a stopping rule, prayer, rest, and a weekly review of whom the work served.

**Pastoral guardrails:** Do not treat illness, disability, neurodivergence, unemployment, poverty, caregiving overload, grief, or clinical depression as laziness. Spiritual practice complements but does not replace medical or psychological care.
**Primary source pack:** Catechism 2427-2428 and 2184-2188; Pope Francis, General Audience on Acedia, 14 February 2024; *Gaudete et Exsultate* on holiness in ordinary life; Catholic social teaching on the dignity of work; biblical wisdom concerning labor, rest, and perseverance.

## Counter-Virtue Course - Patience and Meekness against Wrath

**Why it matters:** Anger can register a real or perceived wrong, but wrath permits anger to harden into vengeance, contempt, indiscriminate aggression, brooding, or hatred. Patience and meekness govern strength so that a person can pursue truth, reconciliation, protection, and justice without becoming ruled by rage.
**Outcome:** The learner can distinguish the passion of anger from the vice of wrath and from righteous indignation, interrupt escalation and brooding, practice patience and meekness, pursue forgiveness and reconciliation truthfully, and take firm action against injustice without hatred.

## Lesson plan

1. **Anger, wrath, and moral responsibility**
   - Anger is a passion and is not automatically sinful. Wrath begins when anger is deliberately fed or expressed contrary to reason and charity. Distinguish the onset of emotion from consent, development, intention, and action.
   - **Scripture:** Ephesians 4:25-32; James 1:19-21; Psalm 4:4-5.
   - **Practice:** Review one episode by separating trigger, bodily reaction, interpretation, chosen response, and consequence.

2. **How wrath grows**
   - Examine humiliation, entitlement, repeated mental prosecution, global judgments, displaced anger, revenge fantasies, hostile speech, and the way wrath can punish uninvolved people. Time alone does not heal anger that is continually rehearsed.
   - **Scripture:** Genesis 4:1-16; Sirach 27:30-28:7; Proverbs 29:22.
   - **Practice:** Identify one thought or behavior that keeps an anger alive and replace it with a truthful statement and a concrete next step.

3. **Righteous indignation is not wrath**
   - A morally ordered anger can respond to injustice and move a person toward protection or correction. Its object, intention, means, proportion, and duration must remain governed by prudence, justice, and charity.
   - **Scripture:** Mark 3:1-6; John 2:13-17; Nehemiah 5:1-13.
   - **Practice:** Test one anger: What wrong is at issue, whose good is sought, and what proportionate action would serve justice rather than revenge?

4. **Patience and meekness: strength under right rule**
   - Patience endures difficult good through time; meekness governs the irascible power rather than abolishing it. Neither is cowardice. Both make room for clear judgment and action free from contempt.
   - **Scripture:** Matthew 5:5; Matthew 11:28-30; Colossians 3:12-15.
   - **Practice:** Rehearse one calm, firm sentence that names a problem without attacking a person's worth.

5. **God's patience and Christ's strength**
   - God's slowness to anger creates room for conversion without calling evil good. Jesus combines endurance with teaching, rebuke, withdrawal, forgiveness, and the steadfastness of the Passion.
   - **Scripture:** Exodus 34:4-9; Luke 22:39-46; 1 Peter 2:19-25.
   - **Practice:** Choose whether one present conflict calls for silence, explanation, correction, boundary, withdrawal, forgiveness, or help from proper authority.

6. **Patience with the weakness of others**
   - Distinguish patient accompaniment from enabling. Consider children, elders, coworkers, family, newcomers, and people who learn or communicate differently. Expectations can remain real while help and time are proportionate.
   - **Scripture:** Romans 15:1-7; 1 Thessalonians 5:12-15; Galatians 6:1-2.
   - **Practice:** Adjust one instruction, pace, or support so another person has a fair chance to succeed.

7. **Patience with oneself and gradual conversion**
   - Growth normally includes repetition, setbacks, and hidden progress. Reject despair and complacency together: receive mercy, learn from failure, and return to the next faithful act.
   - **Scripture:** Philippians 1:3-11; Micah 7:7-9; Luke 13:6-9.
   - **Practice:** Conduct a brief failure review: truth, trigger, responsibility, mercy, repair, and next step—without self-insult.

8. **Suffering, grief, illness, and unanswered questions**
   - Do not use patience to offer easy explanations for suffering. Teach lament, presence, endurance one day at a time, honest prayer, community support, and professional care. The learner is not required to call pain good.
   - **Scripture:** Job 3; Psalm 22; Romans 12:9-15; John 11:17-44.
   - **Practice:** Offer one act of presence to a suffering person without advice, comparison, or forced optimism.

9. **When patience must act**
   - Patience never requires remaining in abuse, delaying emergency aid, tolerating exploitation, or postponing a clear duty indefinitely. The persistent widow shows patient perseverance through action.
   - **Scripture:** Luke 18:1-8; Ecclesiastes 3:1-8; Proverbs 31:8-9.
   - **Practice:** Identify whether one prolonged problem calls for more time, a deadline, a boundary, outside authority, or immediate safety action.

10. **Capstone: a plan for faithful waiting**
   - Integrate hope, lament, controllable action, limits, support, prayer, rest, signs for reassessment, and mercy toward oneself and others.
   - **Scripture:** Romans 5:1-5; Hebrews 10:32-39; Psalm 27.
   - **Capstone:** Build a four-week faithful-waiting plan for one real situation, including an action rhythm and an explicit threshold for seeking help or changing course.

**Pastoral guardrails:** Never use patience to keep a person in danger or to silence grief, protest, reporting, treatment, or lawful action. Do not promise that sufficient faith will produce a preferred outcome on a preferred schedule.
**Primary source pack:** Catechism 1762-1775, 1829, 1832, 2262, and 2302-2303; Pope Francis, General Audiences on Wrath, 31 January 2024, and Patience, 27 March 2024; *Gaudete et Exsultate* 112-121; the biblical traditions of righteous indignation, reconciliation, mercy, and steadfastness.

## Counter-Virtue Course - Kindness and Goodwill against Envy

**Why it matters:** Envy grieves at another person's good and can grow into detraction, rivalry, resentment, or joy at another's loss. Kindness and goodwill train the heart to recognize the other as a brother or sister, rejoice in grace, honor genuine good, and seek another's flourishing through concrete love.
**Outcome:** The learner can recognize envy beneath comparison and resentment, practice gratitude and goodwill, rejoice in another's good without denying personal sorrow, communicate with kindness and truth, resist favoritism and detraction, and form habits that build fraternal communion.

## Lesson plan

1. **Envy: sorrow at another's good**
   - Define envy carefully and distinguish it from admiration, grief over one's own lack, or just concern about unfairness. Envy interprets another's flourishing as a threat and can desire that the good be diminished.
   - **Scripture:** Genesis 4:1-16; Genesis 37:1-36; Wisdom 2:21-24.
   - **Practice:** Name one comparison honestly: the other person's good, the sorrow or fear it awakens, and the good response still possible.

2. **God's generosity and gratitude for one's own gifts**
   - Envy often rests on a false image of God as ungenerous or on the belief that another person's good subtracts from one's own. Gratitude receives gifts without turning them into rank and can lament real lack without accusing the neighbor.
   - **Scripture:** Matthew 20:1-16; Psalm 139:13-18; James 1:16-18.
   - **Practice:** Give thanks for three particular gifts or responsibilities without comparing them to anyone else's.

3. **Rejoicing in another's good**
   - Catholic teaching answers envy with goodwill, humility, and fraternal love. Rejoicing is not pretending that one's own life is painless; it is refusing to make another's gift into an offense.
   - **Scripture:** Romans 12:9-16; Luke 1:39-45; John 3:22-30.
   - **Practice:** Congratulate, bless, or thank one person for a real good without redirecting attention toward oneself.

4. **Kindness as a fruit and expression of charity**
   - Kindness is charity made perceptible: benevolent in motive, respectful in manner, and concrete in action. It opposes envy by actively willing and serving another person's true good.
   - **Scripture:** Galatians 5:22-26; Titus 3:3-8; 1 Thessalonians 5:11-18.
   - **Practice:** Offer one specific encouragement that names a real good and whom it served.

5. **Practical care and hospitality**
   - Kindness notices bodily and social needs: food, transport, welcome, information, accompaniment, access, shelter, rest, and help with burdens. Good help asks rather than assumes and respects agency.
   - **Scripture:** Luke 10:25-37; Hebrews 13:1-3; Romans 12:13.
   - **Practice:** Ask one person, “What would be useful?” and offer one feasible form of care.

6. **Truthful kindness and healthy boundaries**
   - Kindness is compatible with “no,” correction, consequence, and distance. False kindness avoids short-term discomfort while permitting confusion or harm. The manner and purpose of truth matter.
   - **Scripture:** Matthew 5:33-37; Ephesians 4:15; Proverbs 27:5-6.
   - **Practice:** Communicate one small boundary clearly, briefly, and without contempt or excessive justification.

7. **Conflict, gentleness, and repair**
   - Teach de-escalation, curiosity, acknowledgment, apology, restitution, and beginning again. Gentleness governs strength; it does not erase the issue or require agreement.
   - **Scripture:** Galatians 6:1-5; 2 Timothy 2:22-26; Matthew 5:21-26.
   - **Practice:** Make one complete apology: name the act, its impact, responsibility, repair, and intended change—without an excuse disguised as context.

8. **Kindness beyond the familiar**
   - Christian kindness crosses boundaries of class, race, nationality, age, ability, religion, reputation, and usefulness. Examine favoritism and the temptation to reserve warmth for people who can repay it.
   - **Scripture:** Luke 6:27-36; James 2:1-9; Leviticus 19:33-34.
   - **Practice:** Extend dignified welcome or assistance to one person outside the learner's usual social circle.

9. **Hidden service without performance or control**
   - Kindness can be corrupted by image management, indebtedness, rescuing, intrusion, or burnout. Study secrecy, consent, shared responsibility, and service grounded in prayer rather than the need to be needed.
   - **Scripture:** Matthew 6:1-4; John 13:1-17; Mark 10:42-45.
   - **Practice:** Offer one hidden service with no announcement and no expectation of emotional repayment.

10. **Capstone: building a culture of kindness**
   - Integrate attention, speech, encouragement, hospitality, boundaries, repair, inclusion, hidden service, and rest. Move from random acts to dependable patterns in home, parish, work, or community.
   - **Scripture:** Colossians 3:12-17; Hebrews 10:23-25; 1 John 3:16-18.
   - **Capstone:** Design and test a two-week kindness rhythm containing one daily interpersonal practice, one weekly work of mercy, and one repair practice.

**Primary source pack:** Catechism 1829, 1832, 2447, and 2538-2540; Benedict XVI, *Deus Caritas Est*; Pope Francis, General Audience on Envy and Vainglory, 28 February 2024; *Gaudete et Exsultate* on holiness in ordinary relationships; Gospel encounters in which Christ sees, welcomes, serves, and restores.

## Counter-Virtue Course - Humility against Pride

**Why it matters:** Pride exalts the self out of truthful relationship with God and neighbor, seeking independence, superiority, recognition, or control. Humility is its contrary because humility restores truth: God is God, every good is gift, every person possesses dignity, and each creature has real gifts and real limits.
**Outcome:** The learner can recognize pride beyond obvious boasting, distinguish humility from self-contempt, receive gifts gratefully, admit limits and wrongs, accept help and correction, serve without status-seeking, combine humility with courageous responsibility, and form a truthful rule of life.

## Lesson plan

1. **Pride and humility: distortion and truth**
   - Pride is more than visible boasting. It can appear as self-sufficiency, contempt, entitlement, refusal of correction, control of one's image, or the demand to occupy the center. Humility neither deifies the self nor despises what God has made: the person is both dust and God's image.
   - **Scripture:** Genesis 3:1-13; Sirach 10:12-18; James 4:6-10.
   - **Practice:** Name one genuine gift, one genuine limit, and one place where protecting self-importance obstructs truth or love.

2. **Dependence, grace, and prayer**
   - Prayer begins by recognizing that life and holiness cannot be self-produced. Humility receives mercy, asks, listens, and returns thanks; it does not bargain from imagined self-sufficiency.
   - **Scripture:** Luke 18:9-14; John 15:1-8; Psalm 131.
   - **Practice:** Pray one honest request that names need without dramatizing or hiding it.

3. **The humility of Christ**
   - Contemplate the Incarnation, hidden life, service, obedience, Passion, and exaltation of Christ. His humility is strong, purposeful self-gift, not denial of identity or mission.
   - **Scripture:** Philippians 2:1-11; Matthew 11:25-30; John 13:1-17.
   - **Practice:** Choose one low-visibility act of service that fits an actual responsibility.

4. **Mary: lowliness, courage, and praise**
   - Mary's humility receives an immense vocation, asks a truthful question, consents freely, acts promptly, and magnifies God. She does not deny what God has done in her.
   - **Scripture:** Luke 1:26-56; John 2:1-11.
   - **Practice:** Receive one responsibility with both dependence on grace and a concrete first act.

5. **Gifts, competence, and magnanimity**
   - Humility and magnanimity belong together. Gifts are entrusted for service, so hiding them through fear can be as self-focused as boasting. Excellence becomes humble when it remains grateful, teachable, and ordered to the common good.
   - **Scripture:** Matthew 25:14-30; 1 Corinthians 4:1-7; Romans 12:3-8.
   - **Practice:** Use one real ability deliberately for another person's good and credit the help and formation that made it possible.

6. **Correction, repentance, apology, and learning**
   - The humble person can examine evidence, admit wrong, repair harm, and learn without collapsing identity. Not every accusation is true, so humility also tests correction prudently.
   - **Scripture:** Proverbs 12:1, 15; Luke 22:54-62; Psalm 51.
   - **Practice:** Receive one piece of feedback by restating it accurately, asking one question, and choosing an appropriate response.

7. **Asking for and receiving help**
   - Self-sufficiency can conceal fear, pride, or shame. Interdependence lets others exercise their gifts and acknowledges that the Body of Christ has many members.
   - **Scripture:** Exodus 17:8-13; Galatians 6:1-5; 1 Corinthians 12:12-27.
   - **Practice:** Make one clear, proportionate request for help and receive the answer without manipulation.

8. **Status, comparison, and hidden service**
   - Explore honor, recognition, titles, social comparison, defensiveness, and the need to control how one is perceived. Humility can accept thanks without feeding on praise and can serve without being seen.
   - **Scripture:** Luke 14:7-14; Matthew 6:1-6; Mark 9:33-37.
   - **Practice:** Complete one needed task anonymously or without mentioning it afterward.

9. **Humble leadership and courageous speech**
   - Humility does not require silence in the face of error or refusing legitimate authority. A humble leader listens, decides, takes responsibility, gives credit, protects the vulnerable, and speaks truth without self-exaltation.
   - **Scripture:** Numbers 12:3; Mark 10:35-45; Acts 4:13-20.
   - **Practice:** In one decision, seek counsel, state the reason, take responsibility, and credit another person's contribution.

10. **False humility, humiliation, and a rule of truth**
   - Distinguish humility from self-hatred, chronic apology, refusal of praise, fear of visibility, imposed humiliation, and accepting abuse. Integrate dignity, gifts, limits, sin, grace, service, courage, and joy.
   - **Scripture:** Sirach 3:17-24; Luke 1:46-55; 2 Corinthians 12:7-10.
   - **Capstone:** Write a four-week rule of truth: gratitude for gifts, one learning posture, one form of hidden service, one courageous responsibility, and one boundary against false humility.

**Pastoral guardrails:** Humility must never be used to demand submission to abuse, erase a person's voice, excuse exploitation, or intensify shame. Trauma-informed care may be necessary when “be humble” has been weaponized.
**Primary source pack:** Catechism 2540 and 2559-2561; Pope Francis, General Audiences on Pride, 6 March 2024, and Humility, 22 May 2024; Rickaby chapter XI on humility and magnanimity as a supporting historical source; *Gaudete et Exsultate* 67-74 and 112-121; Philippians 2, the Magnificat, and Christ's teaching on service.

## Counter-Virtue Course - Temperance against Gluttony

**Why it matters:** Gluttony disorders a good and necessary appetite. It is not simply enjoying food, celebrating, feeling hunger, or having a particular body size. It makes consumption, selectiveness, quantity, urgency, or comfort rule the person and can widen into wasteful consumption of the earth's goods. Temperance restores gratitude, freedom, proportion, and concern for others.
**Outcome:** The learner can distinguish nourishment and celebration from gluttony, recognize several forms of disordered consumption without judging bodies, practice gratitude and fitting measure, understand Catholic fasting and abstinence, connect personal appetite with justice and creation, and seek appropriate help when eating or drinking involves medical illness, addiction, or compulsion.

## Lesson plan

1. **Food as gift, need, pleasure, and communion**
   - Begin with creation's goodness, dependence upon food, shared tables, biblical feasts, hospitality, and Christ eating with others. The virtue starts from gratitude, not disgust toward appetite or the body.
   - **Scripture:** Genesis 1:29-31; Psalm 104:14-15; John 2:1-11.
   - **Practice:** Receive one meal slowly enough to notice gift, labor, dependence, and companionship.

2. **What gluttony is—and is not**
   - Define gluttony as a disordered relationship to eating, drinking, or consumption. Distinguish vice from hunger, enjoyment, cultural celebration, medical need, body shape, disability, poverty, or an isolated imprudent choice.
   - **Scripture:** Philippians 3:17-21; Sirach 37:27-31; Mark 7:14-23.
   - **Practice:** Examine one pattern by its freedom, purpose, circumstances, effects, and care for others rather than by appearance.

3. **The many forms of voracity**
   - Gluttony can concern quantity, haste, insistence on delicacy, anxious control, constant preoccupation, intoxication, waste, or consuming simply to numb distress. The common issue is appetite escaping right reason and charity.
   - **Scripture:** Numbers 11:4-6, 18-23, 31-34; Proverbs 23:19-21; Luke 16:19-31.
   - **Practice:** Identify the form, trigger, apparent reward, deeper cost, and unmet need in one recurring pattern.

4. **Temperance and the freedom to choose a fitting measure**
   - Apply the cardinal virtue directly: temperance moderates attraction to pleasure and balances the use of created goods. The fitting measure considers health, vocation, duties, hospitality, scandal, resources, and individual vulnerability.
   - **Scripture:** 1 Corinthians 6:12-13; Titus 2:11-14; Proverbs 25:16.
   - **Practice:** Define “enough” for one ordinary setting and name the greater good that measure protects.

5. **Gratitude and a Eucharistic way of life**
   - Pope Francis contrasts predatory consumption with becoming Eucharistic people capable of giving thanks. Gratitude receives without grabbing and remembers God, growers, workers, cooks, guests, poor neighbors, and creation.
   - **Scripture:** Deuteronomy 8:7-18; Luke 24:28-35; 1 Timothy 4:1-5.
   - **Practice:** Offer thanks that names at least three persons or parts of creation involved in one meal.

6. **Fasting, abstinence, and conversion**
   - Explain Catholic fasting and abstinence inside repentance, prayer, almsgiving, solidarity, and preparation for feasting. Asceticism is not punishment, spiritual competition, or permission to disregard age, health, pregnancy, eating disorders, medication, or legitimate exemption.
   - **Scripture:** Isaiah 58:3-12; Matthew 6:16-18; Acts 13:1-3.
   - **Practice:** Choose a safe, Church-shaped act of abstinence and join it to prayer and a concrete gift to someone in need.

7. **Feasting, hospitality, and shared joy**
   - Temperance makes authentic celebration possible. Study the difference between gratitude and excess, hospitality and display, generosity and pressure, shared joy and using others as an audience for consumption.
   - **Scripture:** Deuteronomy 16:13-15; Luke 14:12-24; Revelation 19:6-9.
   - **Practice:** Plan or participate in one simple shared meal centered on welcome rather than extravagance.

8. **Alcohol, addiction, compulsion, and qualified help**
   - Teach the Church's warning against excess and drunkenness while distinguishing ordinary moral struggle from addiction, eating disorders, trauma responses, and medical conditions. Complete abstinence and recovery support may be necessary; sacramental and pastoral care complement rather than replace treatment.
   - **Scripture:** Proverbs 23:29-35; Ephesians 5:15-20; Mark 2:15-17.
   - **Practice:** If concern exists, complete an honest private screening with a qualified resource and identify a safe person or professional to contact.

9. **Consumer gluttony, waste, and the common good**
   - Extend the vice from food to a voracious posture toward created goods. Consider waste, exploitation, ecological damage, indifference to hunger, and systems that turn persons into consumers. Personal practice should accompany, not replace, justice.
   - **Scripture:** Amos 6:1-7; John 6:1-13; James 5:1-6.
   - **Practice:** Reduce one form of avoidable waste and redirect the saved resource toward nourishment or care for others.

10. **Capstone: a rule of grateful consumption**
   - Integrate nourishment, pleasure, freedom, fitting measure, gratitude, fasting, feasting, hospitality, justice, care for creation, and thresholds for help.
   - **Scripture:** Romans 14:1-23; 1 Corinthians 10:23-33; Psalm 145:14-21.
   - **Capstone:** Write and test a four-week rule containing one meal practice, one safe form of restraint, one act of hospitality or almsgiving, and one weekly review without weighing moral worth or the body.

**Pastoral guardrails:** Never infer gluttony from weight, appearance, appetite, poverty, food insecurity, medical need, or cultural eating practices. Do not prescribe fasting to someone for whom it is medically or psychologically unsafe. Encourage qualified care for disordered eating, substance dependence, or compulsive behavior.
**Primary source pack:** Catechism 1809, 1387, 1434, 2043, and 2288-2291; Pope Francis, General Audience on Gluttony, 10 January 2024; Catholic teaching on fasting and abstinence; Scripture's theology of food, feasting, fasting, Eucharistic gratitude, care for the poor, and stewardship of creation.

# How the courses form one path

The courses should be understandable individually, but they should continuously show that Christian virtue is a connected life rather than a shelf of unrelated traits.

## Recommended full-library sequence

1. **Becoming the Person God Calls You to Be** - supplies the vocabulary and the Catholic map.
2. **Humility against Pride** - restores truthful creaturely dignity, dependence, and service.
3. **Charity and Generosity against Avarice** - turns possession toward stewardship, justice, and love.
4. **Kindness and Goodwill against Envy** - learns gratitude and rejoicing in another's good.
5. **Patience and Meekness against Wrath** - governs anger without extinguishing just action.
6. **Chastity against Lust** - integrates sexuality, friendship, fidelity, and self-gift.
7. **Temperance against Gluttony** - forms gratitude, fitting measure, fasting, hospitality, and responsible consumption.
8. **Diligence against Sloth and Acedia** - forms care, perseverance, faithful work, prayer, and rest.
9. **Cardinal Prudence** - teaches the learner to discern a good and choose fitting means.
10. **Cardinal Justice** - turns attention toward God, neighbor, promise, truth, and the common good.
11. **Cardinal Fortitude** - prepares the learner to remain faithful when the good becomes difficult.
12. **Cardinal Temperance** - gives the complete framework for freedom and order across the appetites and created goods.

This sequence presents the requested seven-sin conversion path first and the cardinal architecture second. It is pedagogical, not a ranking of moral importance. Learners should remain free after orientation to begin a course addressing a present responsibility. The two Temperance courses should cross-reference one another and share vocabulary, but each has its own learning outcome: one addresses gluttony specifically and the other teaches the full cardinal virtue.

## Cross-course connections to make explicit

- **Prudence** directs every other moral virtue by identifying fitting action.
- **Justice** establishes what is owed; **charity** exceeds without canceling justice.
- **Fortitude** sustains justice and charity when action becomes difficult.
- **Temperance** frees attention and desire for the good chosen by prudence.
- **Humility** opposes pride by restoring truth, gratitude, and dependence upon God.
- **Charity expressed in generosity** opposes avarice by turning gifts toward God and neighbor.
- **Kindness and goodwill** oppose envy by honoring and serving another's real good.
- **Patience and meekness** oppose wrath by governing anger and sustaining truthful love.
- **Chastity** opposes lust and belongs under temperance as integration of sexuality and relationship.
- **Temperance against gluttony** applies the cardinal virtue to food, drink, fasting, feasting, and consumption.
- **Diligence and perseverance** oppose sloth or acedia through faithful care for the good.

# Reinforcement that produces understanding rather than mere completion

## Shared beginner glossary

The first appearance of each term should include a tap-to-open definition in ordinary language. The permanent glossary should include at least: beatitude, virtue, vice, habit, moral act, object, intention, circumstance, freedom, responsibility, passion, temptation, consent, conscience, grace, sacrament, cardinal virtue, theological virtue, gift of the Holy Spirit, fruit of the Holy Spirit, common good, restitution, asceticism, acedia, self-mastery, chastity, charity, mercy, forgiveness, reconciliation, magnanimity, and scrupulosity.

## Retrieval and cumulative review

Each lesson should end with three questions:

1. **Recall:** What does the virtue or term mean?
2. **Distinguish:** How is it different from a named counterfeit?
3. **Judge:** What would this virtue notice or do in a realistic situation?

Lessons 3, 6, and 9 of every course should include a five-question cumulative review. Lesson 10 should use a new scenario that requires the learner to combine the course with at least two earlier virtues. Wrong answers should explain the reasoning and link back to the relevant teaching; they should never shame the learner or diagnose character.

## Scenario bank

The finished library needs cases from multiple adult circumstances so Catholic moral formation is not reduced to one personality or season of life:

- marriage, single life, dating, celibacy, widowhood, and family life;
- parenting, adult children, friendship, caregiving, parish life, and difficult relatives;
- employment, management, unemployment, study, retirement, household labor, and volunteering;
- money, possessions, debt, generosity, digital media, food, alcohol, and entertainment;
- illness, disability, neurodivergence, grief, loneliness, recovery, and professional care;
- civic responsibility, social exclusion, injustice, confidentiality, reporting, and proper authority.

No case should imply that vocation, health, wealth, productivity, marital status, or temperament establishes a person's holiness.

## Misconception matrix

Every course should repeatedly reject its most persuasive counterfeit:

| Virtue | Not the same as |
|---|---|
| Prudence | timidity, indecision, calculation, or clever self-interest |
| Justice | revenge, legalism, cold fairness, or charity without restitution |
| Fortitude | fearlessness, aggression, rashness, or remaining in danger |
| Temperance | body contempt, joylessness, rigid control, or one measure for everyone |
| Chastity | repression, shame, prudishness, marital status, or absence of attraction |
| Charity | niceness, sentimentality, enabling, control, or approval of everything |
| Diligence | busyness, perfectionism, constant availability, or worth through output |
| Patience | passivity, denial, indefinite delay, or tolerance of abuse |
| Kindness | people-pleasing, flattery, conflict avoidance, intrusion, or performance |
| Humility | self-hatred, incompetence, silence, imposed humiliation, or refusal of gifts |

## Practice and review after a course

The optional 30-day practice period should use the learner's capstone rather than add doctrine. A humane rhythm is:

- **Daily:** one small practice and one sentence of prayer.
- **Weekly:** one five-minute review—where did grace, resistance, failure, repair, or help appear?
- **Day 15:** revise the practice if it is unsafe, vague, impossible, or no longer serves the intended good.
- **Day 30:** write a brief testimony of learning and select either continued practice, a different virtue course, or a conversation with a priest, mentor, clinician, recovery resource, or other qualified person.

The review should measure participation and learning, not claim to measure the possession of virtue. Streaks may support memory, but a missed day must not be represented as moral failure.

# What the learner should see before enrolling

Every course preview should answer six questions honestly:

1. **Why might this matter in my life?** A short ordinary-life problem, not a guilt-based sales pitch.
2. **What will I learn?** The course outcome in plain language.
3. **What will I not be promised?** No instant transformation, moral diagnosis, or replacement for pastoral or professional care.
4. **How is the course organized?** Ten named lessons, estimated lesson length, five-week suggested pace, and optional 30-day practice.
5. **What does the Church teach?** A short source note naming the Catechism sections and principal Catholic document.
6. **What will I be asked to do?** Samples of Scripture study, case judgment, prayer, and a concrete but revisable practice.

The section landing page should open with **Why Virtue?**, then **Start Here**, followed by **Seven Virtues against the Seven Capital Sins** and **The Four Cardinal Virtues**. Temperance should be visibly present in both tracks. The counter-vice course focuses specifically on gluttony; the cardinal course teaches Temperance's complete moral scope. Both must make clear that they concern the same virtue.

# Content-authoring standard

The blueprint above is complete enough to define the course architecture, but each lesson still requires a full authored manuscript before publication. Every manuscript should include:

1. lesson title, opening question, and single learning goal;
2. 800-1,200 words of original teaching in beginner language;
3. no more than four defined terms;
4. one primary Scripture passage treated in context and no more than two supporting references;
5. one example from Christ, a biblical witness, or the life of the Church;
6. one explicit misconception and why it is attractive;
7. one realistic case with an explained response—not merely an answer key;
8. one small, safe, revisable practice;
9. three retrieval questions and a one-sentence takeaway;
10. a short original prayer;
11. paragraph-level source notes for doctrinal or historical claims;
12. a pastoral note whenever the topic intersects abuse, trauma, compulsion, mental illness, medical needs, scrupulosity, or legal/safety concerns.

## Definition of content-ready

A lesson is ready for implementation only after all of the following are true:

- every doctrinal claim is mapped to Scripture, the Catechism, a council or papal document, or another authoritative Catholic source;
- Scripture has been checked in context and the app links to an authorized translation rather than reproducing unlicensed text;
- the prose assumes no theological background and unexplained jargon has been removed;
- the lesson distinguishes the virtue from its counterfeit and avoids moralizing temperament, health, disability, poverty, or life state;
- the practice is specific, proportionate, optional, and safe;
- the case study includes reasoning across object, intention, circumstances, responsibilities, and foreseeable effects where relevant;
- the lesson has received theological review and pastoral/sensitivity review appropriate to its topic;
- facts, quotations, links, and document numbering have passed editorial verification.

# Catholic source hierarchy and research library

The curriculum should be authored from a clear hierarchy rather than from unattributed devotional summaries.

## Tier 1 - Normative foundation

- Sacred Scripture in an authorized Catholic edition.
- *Catechism of the Catholic Church*, especially 1691-1876, 1996-2046, 2184-2188, 2288-2291, 2337-2359, 2426-2449, 2514-2565.
- The documents of the Second Vatican Council, especially *Lumen Gentium* 39-42 on the universal call to holiness.

## Tier 2 - Papal formation and application

- Francis, *Gaudete et Exsultate*, especially its treatment of ordinary holiness, the Beatitudes, perseverance, community, prayer, and discernment.
- Benedict XVI, *Deus Caritas Est*, for God's prior love, the unity of embodied and self-giving love, and the relationship among Church, Eucharist, justice, and charity.
- Pope Francis's 2024 General Audiences on vices and virtues, especially prudence, patience, justice, fortitude, temperance, charity, humility, acedia, lust, and gluttony.

## Tier 3 - Historical theological companion

- Joseph Rickaby, S.J., *Four-Square; or, The Cardinal Virtues* (1908), used for conceptual depth, the unity of the virtues, practical distinctions, religion under justice, and humility with magnanimity.

Rickaby is a supporting guide, not doctrine, law, or the curriculum's final authority. No doctrinal or moral claim should rest on Rickaby alone. Scripture, the Catechism, conciliar teaching, and the living papal Magisterium govern; if a historical formulation conflicts with or fails to reflect those sources, the authoritative Catholic source controls. Rickaby's useful philosophical distinctions should be translated into current language, and dated examples, assumptions, polemical rhetoric, or pastoral judgments should not be copied merely because they are historical.

## Scripture architecture

Each course should have a primary biblical spine rather than a collage of proof texts:

- **Orientation:** Beatitudes; life in Christ; freedom, habit, grace, gifts, and fruits.
- **Prudence:** biblical wisdom, Solomon, Christ's teaching on watchfulness and discernment.
- **Justice:** covenant fidelity, prophets, truth and restitution, Christ's concern for the excluded.
- **Fortitude:** Esther, Susanna, prophets, apostles, Passion, and martyrs' witness.
- **Cardinal Temperance:** creation's goodness, wisdom on appetite, self-mastery, balance, and freedom in Christ.
- **Chastity against Lust:** creation, covenant, body, fidelity, friendship, and self-gift.
- **Charity and Generosity against Avarice:** God's prior love, stewardship, almsgiving, works of mercy, the poor, and the common life of the Church.
- **Diligence against Sloth or Acedia:** creation stewardship, wisdom on work, Christ's hidden and public labor, Sabbath, and perseverance.
- **Patience and Meekness against Wrath:** anger and righteous indignation, divine mercy, forgiveness, reconciliation, Christ's Passion, and just action.
- **Kindness and Goodwill against Envy:** Cain and Abel, gratitude, rejoicing in another's good, encouragement, fraternal honor, and service.
- **Humility against Pride:** creation, Fall, Incarnation, Magnificat, foot washing, Cross, service, limits, and gifts.
- **Temperance against Gluttony:** food as gift, Israel's wilderness, fasting, feasting, Eucharistic gratitude, hospitality, restraint, and care for the poor and creation.

# Editorial and pastoral guardrails for the entire library

1. **Grace before technique.** Never imply that an optimized habit system can manufacture holiness.
2. **Truth without shame.** Name sin and responsibility clearly while preserving dignity, mercy, gradual growth, and the distinction among temptation, consent, action, habit, and culpability.
3. **No spiritualized danger.** Patience, charity, humility, chastity, forgiveness, obedience, or fortitude must never be used to keep someone in abuse, prevent reporting, or replace emergency, legal, medical, psychological, or recovery help.
4. **No scrupulosity engine.** Avoid exhaustive self-surveillance, holiness scores, sin predictions, forced confessions to the app, or alerts that frame missed practice as disobedience.
5. **No productivity gospel.** Work, discipline, streaks, and completion never determine human worth.
6. **No body contempt.** Temperance and chastity begin from creation's goodness and integration, not disgust toward appetite, pleasure, sexuality, illness, age, or disability.
7. **No counterfeit reconciliation.** Forgiveness can coexist with grief, consequences, reporting, restitution, distance, and the absence of restored trust.
8. **Respect state and capacity.** Examples must include different vocations, ages, health conditions, responsibilities, cultures, and material circumstances without presenting one as the universal adult norm.
9. **Use Church terms precisely.** Keep the four cardinal virtues, three theological virtues, gifts, fruits, seven capital sins, and the seven-course counter-vice formation track distinct.
10. **Review before publication.** A priest, qualified Catholic moral theologian, or catechetical reviewer should verify doctrine; relevant clinicians or safeguarding experts should review lessons involving trauma, abuse, addiction, compulsions, or mental illness.

# Relationship to the material already in the app

The existing short foundation lessons and four-lesson virtue paths contain useful beginnings: definitions, Scripture links, reflection prompts, and small practices. They should be treated as source notes and early prototypes, not discarded. The extended curriculum changes their role:

- the current foundation becomes the research base for the eleven-lesson foundation course;
- the current cardinal introductions expand into full five-week courses;
- the current daily virtue lessons become seeds for selected lessons and post-course practices;
- Temperance is taught in two coordinated courses: a focused remedy to gluttony and the complete cardinal framework, without pretending there are two different virtues;
- tracking changes from “virtue completed” to “lesson studied” and “practice reviewed” whenever implementation eventually occurs.

No application code or data model change is part of this blueprint.

# Final curriculum outcome

A beginner who completes the full path should be able to:

- explain why Catholic moral life is ordered toward beatitude, holiness, and love rather than mere rule compliance;
- distinguish the four cardinal virtues, three theological virtues, gifts and fruits of the Spirit, seven capital sins, and the curriculum's seven counter-virtue courses;
- explain each virtue in plain language and recognize its major counterfeits;
- interpret common life situations with greater attention to truth, duties, freedom, desire, difficulty, relationship, and grace;
- use Scripture, prayer, sacraments, counsel, community, deliberate practice, repentance, and repair as parts of one formation process;
- choose a small, fitting practice without confusing completion with possession of virtue;
- know when a difficulty requires pastoral counsel, community support, professional care, safeguarding, or proper authority;
- continue growing after the last lesson through revisable rules of life, periodic review, mercy after failure, and deeper conformity to Christ.

The governing promise of the entire section should be modest and true: **These courses cannot make a person holy by themselves. They can teach the Catholic map, train attention and judgment, propose faithful practice, and continually direct the learner toward Christ, grace, the Church, and a lifetime of love.**
